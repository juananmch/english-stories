# The Resignation Letter

Not once in fourteen years had Eleanor seriously considered leaving the firm.
Rarely does a career afford the kind of stability she had built there, and
she was, by any reasonable measure, comfortable — well compensated, broadly
respected, insulated from the precariousness that seemed to define so many
of her peers' working lives elsewhere. That comfort, however, had become
increasingly difficult to reconcile with what she now knew.

It had started with a set of figures that didn't add up, tucked into a
quarterly report she'd been asked to review as a formality more than
anything. Had she not happened to cross-reference them against an earlier
draft, the discrepancy might well have gone unnoticed indefinitely. Once
noticed, though, it could not be unnoticed, and the more she examined it, the
clearer the pattern became: revenue figures had been, not fabricated
outright, but massaged into a shape more flattering than the underlying
reality warranted.

She raised it, initially, through what she considered the appropriate
channel — a private conversation with her direct manager, Julian, whom she
had trusted implicitly until that conversation. His response was neither
denial nor alarm, but something closer to weary resignation of his own.

"This isn't new," he told her, not quite meeting her eyes. "It's been
happening for years. Everyone above a certain level knows. The expectation,
frankly, is that you'll learn to live with it, the way the rest of us have."

That the practice was neither new nor secret struck her as, if anything,
more troubling than if it had been an isolated lapse. An isolated lapse might
be corrected. A culture, by contrast, tends to entrench itself, and those who
raise objections within it are seldom thanked for the effort.

She spent several weeks deliberating, weighing what she owed the
institution that had shaped so much of her professional identity against what
she owed to something less easily named — a standard she held for herself
that had nothing to do with loyalty to any particular employer. Notwithstanding
the very real financial cost of walking away from a role of that seniority,
she found she could not, in good conscience, continue signing off on figures
she knew to be misleading.

Her resignation letter, when she finally wrote it, was notably brief. She
had drafted longer versions — versions that itemized every grievance,
every complicity she had nearly been drawn into — before discarding them in
favor of two sentences that stated, plainly, that she was leaving, and that
she would be available to discuss the reasons with anyone in a position to
act on them.

Whether anyone ever did act on them, she never learned. What she did learn,
in the months that followed, was that the discomfort of unemployment, however
real, proved considerably easier to live with than the alternative would
have been.

She found work eventually, at a smaller firm with considerably less
prestige attached to its name, and was asked, in the course of her interview
there, why she had left her previous position after so many years. She gave
the honest answer, expecting it to count against her. It did not. Her new
employer, as it turned out, had encountered the same developer's figures
from the other side of a negotiation some years earlier and had drawn his
own conclusions at the time, conclusions he had lacked the standing to act
on. Julian, so far as she has heard, remains at the original firm still.
