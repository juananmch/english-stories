# The Last Deal

At seventy-one, Julian had long since made peace with the notion that his
most consequential business decisions lay behind him rather than ahead — a
reasonable enough conclusion, given that he had sold his controlling stake in
the company he'd founded a decade earlier and had, since then, occupied
himself with the comfortable irrelevance of a nonexecutive board seat. It was
against this backdrop of settled expectation that the phone call from his
former protégée, Priya, arrived, disrupting an equilibrium he had not
realized how thoroughly he'd come to depend on.

She was launching a venture, she explained, in a sector adjacent to the one
that had made his own reputation, and wanted, before approaching institutional
investors, his read on whether the underlying premise held up to scrutiny.
Not once in the eleven years since he'd sold the company had anyone sought
his judgment with quite that degree of earnestness, and he found himself,
somewhat to his own surprise, flattered in a manner he hadn't anticipated
being susceptible to any longer.

He read the materials she sent with a thoroughness that surprised even him,
returning to sections he'd already reviewed, cross-referencing assumptions
against instincts honed over three decades in the industry. What emerged
was a picture considerably more promising than he'd expected: a genuine
insight, competently executed, undermined by exactly the kind of naive
assumptions about scaling that had derailed ventures far better funded than
this one looked likely to be.

"You're not wrong about the opportunity," he told her, when they finally
spoke. "You are, however, dangerously wrong about how quickly you'll be
able to capture it. Had I made the assumptions you've made here, at your
stage, I would have run out of runway eighteen months before profitability,
and I would have deserved to."

She took the criticism, he noted with something approaching pride, without
the defensiveness he half expected from someone still early enough in her
career to mistake conviction for correctness. They spent the following three
hours reworking her projections line by line, an exercise that reminded him,
uncomfortably and not unpleasantly, of exactly how much he had once enjoyed
this particular species of problem.

When she asked, toward the end of the evening, whether he would consider
joining as an informal advisor, he found himself accepting with a readiness
that startled him. He had told himself, for years, that he had stepped back
by choice, that the settled comfort of the board seat suited a man his age
rather better than the churn of building something new. Only now, confronted
with the prospect of doing precisely that once more, did he recognize how
much of that settlement had been resignation dressed up, not altogether
convincingly, as contentment.

He mentioned the arrangement to his wife over dinner that evening, half
expecting her to voice the concerns about overextension she had raised when
he'd first stepped back from the company years earlier. She said nothing of
the sort. "You've been restless for at least a year," she told him, with the
particular bluntness of someone who has simply been waiting for a
conversation to catch up with an observation she'd already made. "I was
beginning to wonder how long it would take you to notice it yourself."

## New Words

- **protégée** — a person guided and supported by a more experienced mentor
- **equilibrium** — a state of balance
- **premise** — a basic assumption on which an argument or plan is built
- **earnestness** — sincere and serious intent
- **runway** (business) — the time before a company runs out of money
- **defensiveness** — a tendency to protect oneself against criticism
- **contentment** — a state of peaceful satisfaction
