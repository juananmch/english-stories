# A Question of Loyalty

It was essential, Priya's lawyer insisted, that she be scrupulously honest in
her deposition, regardless of whom the honesty implicated. What the lawyer
could not have anticipated was quite how personal that implication would
turn out to be.

The case concerned allegations of financial mismanagement at the small
publishing house where Priya had worked for six years — allegations that,
as the investigation progressed, pointed with increasing specificity toward
Simon, the company's co-founder and, not incidentally, one of her closest
friends. She had known him since university, had stood as a witness at his
wedding, had spent countless evenings at his kitchen table long before either
of them had any stake in the business that now threatened to unravel their
friendship entirely.

Were she a less conscientious person, she might have found it easier to
plead a convenient ignorance — to claim she had simply never noticed the
irregularities that, in retrospect, seemed glaringly obvious. That option,
however, was never seriously available to her. She had, in fact, noticed
several things over the years that she had chosen, out of loyalty or
perhaps cowardice, not to examine too closely.

"You don't have to volunteer more than you're asked," her lawyer advised her,
"but you cannot misrepresent what you do know. Were you to do that, you would
be exposing yourself to considerably graver consequences than anything Simon
currently faces."

The deposition itself lasted the better part of a day. She answered every
question as precisely as she could, neither embellishing nor minimizing,
though she found herself, more than once, having to suppress an instinct to
soften facts that could not, in fairness, be softened. Rarely had she felt
so acutely the tension between two obligations that ought, in an ideal world,
never to have come into conflict: her obligation to the truth, and her
obligation to a friendship she had assumed would outlast any circumstance.

It did not, in the end, outlast this one. Simon did not contact her again
after the proceedings concluded, and she found she could not entirely blame
him, even as she remained convinced she had done the only thing her
conscience would permit. There is a particular kind of grief, she came to
understand, reserved for friendships that end not through betrayal exactly,
but through an honesty neither party was equipped to survive intact.

Had circumstances been otherwise — had the truth been kinder to both of
them — she suspects they might still be friends. As it stands, she has made
her peace with a loss that, however necessary, she does not pretend was
painless.

She ran into him once, some eighteen months later, at a mutual friend's
wedding, an encounter neither of them could reasonably have avoided without
drawing more attention to the avoidance than the meeting itself warranted.
They exchanged pleasantries, briefly, with the careful civility of two
people who once knew each other far better than the conversation implied.
Neither mentioned the deposition. She does not know whether he has
forgiven her, in any meaningful sense, and suspects the question may simply
be the wrong one to ask. What she has settled for instead is the more
modest conviction that she would, if the circumstances recurred exactly as
they had, make precisely the same choice again.

## New Words

- **deposition** — formal sworn testimony given as part of a legal case
- **implicate** — to show that someone is involved in something wrong
- **conscientious** — careful to act correctly and honestly
- **irregularity** — something that deviates from a normal, proper pattern
- **glaringly** — very obviously, impossible to miss
- **acutely** — intensely, sharply felt
- **proceedings** — the formal process or actions in a legal case
