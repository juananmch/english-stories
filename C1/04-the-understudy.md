# The Understudy

For two seasons, Eleanor had understudied the lead role without once setting
foot on stage in it, a fact that had, by degrees, curdled her initial
gratitude for the opportunity into something considerably less charitable.
Not until the final week of the run — when the lead, Camille, was felled by
a bout of laryngitis inconvenient enough to be undeniable — did Eleanor
finally receive her chance.

She had, in fairness, anticipated this moment for so long that its actual
arrival felt almost anticlimactic, arriving as it did amid the mundane
chaos of a stage manager's frantic phone calls rather than any of the
dramatic circumstances she had occasionally, guiltily, imagined for it. There
was no time, in the event, for triumph; there was only the immediate, urgent
business of performing a role she knew technically but had never once been
permitted to inhabit before an audience.

The performance itself went, by most objective measures, well. The
reviews the following morning were generous, several noting with evident
surprise that the understudy had brought a freshness to the role the
production's regular lead — though the critics were careful not to phrase
it quite so bluntly — had perhaps allowed to grow slightly routine. Were
Eleanor a less self-aware person, she might have allowed herself unreserved
pleasure in this vindication. As it was, she found the praise complicated
by an awareness of precisely what it implied about the woman whose illness
had made the opportunity possible.

Camille returned for the final two performances, her voice sufficiently
recovered, and said nothing directly about the reviews, though Eleanor
suspected she had read them; it would have been remarkable had she not. Their
interactions during those final days were civil, professionally so, yet
freighted with an unspoken tension neither woman seemed inclined to address
directly.

It was only backstage, on the closing night, that Camille finally broached
it, with a candor Eleanor had not anticipated. "I read what they wrote," she
said. "I won't pretend it didn't sting. But I'd rather know that than spend
the rest of my career wondering whether I'd grown complacent without anyone
having the nerve to tell me."

Eleanor had not expected generosity, still less had she expected to
receive it so directly, and found herself, for a moment, without an
adequate response. What she eventually managed — an acknowledgment that she
had learned as much from watching Camille's performances as from finally
giving her own — was, she felt afterward, inadequate to the moment, though
Camille seemed to receive it as sufficient.

Whether the two of them might have become genuine friends under different
circumstances, Eleanor cannot say with confidence. What she can say is that
the respect that survived that fraught week struck her as, in its own
understated way, considerably rarer than friendship, and no less valuable
for that.

They worked together again, two years later, on a different production,
Camille once more in the lead and Eleanor cast, this time, in a supporting
role she had actively sought rather than merely accepted. Neither of them
mentioned the earlier run directly, though Eleanor suspected it informed,
in ways neither felt the need to articulate, the easy professional shorthand
that had developed between them — the sense, unspoken but evidently mutual,
that each had already witnessed the other at a moment neither could
entirely control, and had emerged from it with something intact.

## New Words

- **understudy** (noun/verb) — a performer trained to replace the lead if
  needed
- **curdle** — to turn sour or unpleasant, like milk going bad
- **charitable** — generous or forgiving in judgment of others
- **anticlimactic** — less exciting than expected after a long buildup
- **inhabit** (a role) — to perform a character with full conviction
- **vindication** — proof that one's judgment or ability was justified
- **freighted** — heavily loaded or burdened, often emotionally
- **broach** — to bring up a difficult subject for discussion
