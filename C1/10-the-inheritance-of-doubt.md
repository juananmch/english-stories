# The Inheritance of Doubt

Julian had promised himself, long before his daughter was born, that he
would raise her without the particular strain of anxious perfectionism his
own father had instilled in him — a promise that had proven, in the years
since, considerably easier to make than to keep. It was not that he had
failed outright; by most external measures, he had succeeded in creating a
household markedly gentler than the one he'd grown up in. What troubled him
was subtler, and harder to name, than outright failure would have been.

It surfaced most clearly in small moments — the reflexive tightening he felt
watching Lucy, his eight-year-old, attempt something new and imperfectly,
the words of encouragement that emerged from him only after a barely
perceptible pause during which some older, less generous instinct had first
to be suppressed. Were an outside observer to watch him closely enough, he
suspects they might notice what he had spent years trying to conceal even
from himself: that the anxiety he'd inherited had not been eliminated so
much as driven underground, where it continued to exert an influence he
could feel but not always control.

The moment that finally forced him to confront this arrived unremarkably
enough. Lucy, having struggled through a piano recital piece she'd
practiced for weeks, made a visible error midway through and, rather than
recovering as her teacher had coached her to, simply stopped, her face
crumpling in a manner that struck him with uncomfortable familiarity. He
recognized, watching her, the exact expression he imagined he himself must
have worn as a child in comparable circumstances — and recognized, with a
jolt considerably less comfortable, his own father's expression as he
recalled it from those same childhood moments: not cruel, exactly, but
disappointed in a way that had somehow felt worse than cruelty would have.

He caught himself, in that instant, on the verge of an instinctive response
he immediately understood he did not want to give. What he said instead,
crouching beside her afterward, was considerably more deliberate than
anything that would previously have occurred to him spontaneously: that
mistakes were not merely tolerable but, properly understood, evidence that
she was attempting something sufficiently difficult to be worth attempting
at all.

Whether this single intervention will prove sufficient to break a pattern
that has, by his own account, persisted across at least two generations, he
cannot honestly claim to know. What he does know, with rather more
confidence, is that patterns inherited unconsciously are considerably harder
to interrupt than patterns recognized and named — and that naming this one,
however belatedly, had felt like the first genuine progress he had made
against it in nearly a decade of otherwise well-intentioned effort.

He called his own father that weekend, an unremarkable enough gesture in
itself, though it had been some time since he had done so without a
specific occasion prompting it. He did not raise the piano recital, nor
anything resembling the pattern he had spent the preceding days examining.
He asked, instead, mundane questions about his father's week, and found,
somewhat to his surprise, that the conversation felt easier than such
conversations between them typically did — as though naming the pattern
privately had already begun, in some small way, to loosen its hold on how
he spoke to the man who had first handed it down to him.

## New Words

- **perfectionism** — an insistence on flawlessness, often to an unhealthy
  degree
- **reflexive** — done automatically, without conscious thought
- **perceptible** — able to be noticed or detected
- **crumple** (of a face) — to visibly collapse into an expression of
  distress
- **jolt** — a sudden, sharp shock or shift in feeling
- **spontaneously** — done naturally, without planning
- **belatedly** — later than would have been ideal
