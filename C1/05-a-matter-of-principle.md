# A Matter of Principle

Simon had built his small architectural practice on a handful of principles
he considered, if not sacred exactly, then at least sufficiently important
to justify the periodic financial sacrifices their observance entailed. Chief
among these was a longstanding refusal to work with developers whose
projects, in his estimation, prioritized profit margins over the communities
those projects would ultimately reshape.

The offer that arrived that spring tested this principle more severely than
any before it. A substantial commission, from a developer whose previous
projects Simon had privately criticized on more than one occasion, would
have resolved the firm's cash flow difficulties for the better part of two
years. Were the offer marginally smaller, he suspects he would have declined
it without a second thought, as he had declined comparable offers before.
Its scale, however, complicated what had previously been, for him, an
uncomplicated calculation.

He discussed it, at length, with his business partner, Julian, whose more
pragmatic temperament had periodically clashed with Simon's over the years,
though never so sharply as it did that week.

"You can afford your principles when the firm isn't three months from
insolvency," Julian told him, with a directness Simon found difficult to
dismiss outright. "If we don't take this, we may not be in a position to
turn down the next developer either, provided there is a next time at all."

The argument was not without merit, and Simon found himself, uncomfortably,
unable to produce an entirely satisfying rebuttal. He had, after all, built
the practice partly to provide livelihoods for the eight people it employed,
several of whom had families depending on those livelihoods. Refusing the
commission on principle was, in that light, a decision with consequences
extending well beyond his own conscience.

He accepted the commission, in the end, though not without negotiating
concessions — modifications to the design that, while falling considerably
short of what he might have insisted upon from a position of greater
leverage, nonetheless represented, he told himself, a meaningful
improvement over what the developer had originally proposed. Whether this
constituted principled compromise or merely a more comfortable species of
capitulation, he has not entirely resolved even now.

What troubles him most, in retrospect, is not the decision itself so much as
how readily he found justifications for it once the alternative grew
sufficiently costly. He does not doubt that Julian was, in the narrow
financial sense, correct. He remains considerably less certain that being
correct, in that sense, settled the question he had actually been asking
himself.

The firm has since recovered its footing, in large part because of that
commission, and Simon has, in the two years since, turned down three
comparable offers from similarly minded developers, each refusal made
somewhat easier by the financial cushion the earlier compromise afforded
him. He is aware of the irony without finding it especially comfortable:
that the exception he made to his principle may be precisely what has
allowed him to keep it, largely intact, ever since.

He raised the matter with Julian only once more, some months later, curious
whether his partner viewed the whole episode the same way in retrospect.
Julian did not. "I'd make the same call again tomorrow," he said, without
particular hesitation. "You're the one who's still turning it over. I think
that's simply the difference between us — I made peace with the compromise
the day we signed it, and you never quite have." Simon suspects this
assessment is accurate, and finds he does not especially want it to change.

## New Words

- **sacred** — regarded as too important to compromise or violate
- **commission** (noun) — a formal request to produce a piece of work
- **insolvency** — the state of being unable to pay one's debts
- **rebuttal** — an argument that responds to and challenges another argument
- **livelihood** — the means by which a person earns enough to live
- **concession** — something given up or allowed in a negotiation
- **capitulation** — the act of giving in after resistance
- **justification** — a reason given to show that something is acceptable

## Questions

1. What principle had Simon built his architectural practice on?
2. Why did this particular offer test that principle more than previous ones?
3. What argument did Julian make in favor of accepting the commission?
4. What did Simon negotiate before accepting the offer?
5. What continues to trouble Simon most about the decision, in retrospect?
