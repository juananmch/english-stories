# The Long Silence

Adrian had not written a publishable page in just over three years, a fact
he had grown practiced at concealing behind vague references to "a new
project" whenever the subject arose at gatherings he could not avoid. The
truth, insofar as he could articulate it even to himself, was less a project
than an absence — a silence that had settled in after his second novel's
reception and had, for reasons he could not entirely account for, refused to
lift.

The novel itself had done reasonably well, by the modest standards of
literary fiction; it was the reviews, or more precisely one review in
particular, that seemed to have lodged itself somewhere he could not
dislodge it from. The critic had not been cruel, exactly — merely, in a
manner that felt almost surgical, precise about the book's limitations,
suggesting that Adrian possessed technical facility in abundance but had yet
to demonstrate he had anything urgent to say with it.

He told himself, at first, that the review was simply one opinion among many,
and largely a positive reception besides. That rationalization sufficed for
perhaps a month. Thereafter, every sentence he attempted seemed to arrive
already anticipating that same critique, preemptively hedged, cautious to
the point of lifelessness. He would delete drafts not because they were bad,
he later came to suspect, but because they were merely competent, and
competence, he had begun to believe, was precisely the accusation he could
least bear to have confirmed a second time.

His partner, Camille, watched this unfold with a patience that, by her own
later admission, occasionally frayed. "You're not blocked," she told him one
evening, with a bluntness she rarely permitted herself on the subject. "You're
frightened. Those aren't the same thing, however much they might resemble
each other from where you're standing."

It was, he would later concede, an uncomfortably accurate diagnosis. Being
blocked implied an obstacle external to himself, whereas being frightened
implicated him rather more directly, and he had not been prepared, at the
time, to accept that degree of implication. Had she said it a year earlier,
he suspects he would have dismissed it outright.

What eventually loosened the silence was not, as he might once have
imagined, some sudden reclamation of confidence, but something closer to
its opposite: a gradual, almost grudging willingness to write badly on
purpose, to produce pages he had no intention of showing anyone, precisely
so that the fear of judgment could find nothing to attach itself to. Only
once he had accumulated a considerable quantity of deliberately unpublishable
material did something resembling his actual voice begin, tentatively, to
reassert itself.

He does not know, even now, whether what follows will meet the standard that
review once implied he had failed to reach. He has, however reluctantly,
made his peace with writing it regardless.

Camille read the first genuinely finished chapter on a Sunday afternoon,
saying very little while she did, which he had long since learned to
interpret as a more promising sign than immediate enthusiasm would have
been. When she finally looked up, she told him only that it sounded like
him again, a remark so modest he nearly missed its significance. It was not
praise, exactly. It was, he came to realize over the following days,
considerably more useful than praise would have been.

## New Words

- **lodge** — to become firmly fixed in a particular place
- **dislodge** — to remove something from where it has become fixed
- **facility** — natural ease or skill in doing something
- **rationalization** — a reasonable-sounding explanation used to justify
  something, often to oneself
- **preemptively** — done in advance to prevent something anticipated
- **hedge** (verb) — to avoid committing fully, to protect against being wrong
- **fray** — to become worn, strained, or less controlled
- **bluntness** — the quality of speaking very directly, without softening
- **diagnosis** — an identification of the nature of a problem
- **reclamation** — the act of getting something back or restoring it
- **grudging** — done reluctantly, without enthusiasm
