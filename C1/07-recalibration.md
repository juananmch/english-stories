# Recalibration

Camille had spent the better part of two decades cultivating an identity so
thoroughly bound up with her profession that she had rarely thought to
examine where the person ended and the surgeon began. It was, accordingly,
a peculiarly disorienting experience to find herself, at forty-six,
signing paperwork that would end her clinical practice permanently, the
tremor in her right hand having progressed to a point beyond which continuing
to operate would have constituted, in her own unsparing assessment, a
genuine hazard to her patients.

Were the diagnosis merely inconvenient, she suspects she might have found
some accommodation, some workaround that preserved at least the shape of
the life she had built. Essential tremor, however, admits no such
compromise where surgical precision is concerned; a hand that could not be
trusted to hold entirely still had no legitimate business inside another
person's body, whatever the years of training behind it.

The weeks following her decision were, she later admitted to her husband,
Adrian, the closest she had come to what she imagined grief must properly
resemble — not for a person, but for a version of herself she had not
realized how completely she'd conflated with her actual identity.
"I don't know who I am," she told him, with a bluntness that startled them
both, "if I'm not the person who does this."

Adrian, who had built his own career around words rather than scalpels, did
not offer the reassurance she suspects a less perceptive partner might have
reached for. "Maybe you don't, yet," he said instead. "That's not the same
as there being nothing there to find."

It was advice easier offered than followed, and the months that ensued
were, by any honest accounting, difficult ones — a slow, often reluctant
process of discovering which parts of her prior identity had been genuinely
her and which had simply been convenient scaffolding erected around a
profession that no longer existed for her to inhabit. She found, somewhat to
her own surprise, that her capacity for exacting attention — the very trait
that had made her an excellent surgeon — transferred rather more readily
than she'd anticipated to an entirely different pursuit: teaching, first
informally, then through a formal appointment training the next generation
of surgical residents in precisely the judgment, if no longer the manual
dexterity, that had defined her own career.

She does not claim the transition was seamless, nor that she has fully
resolved what she lost in making it. What she has come to believe, with
something approaching confidence, is that had she clung more stubbornly to
the identity she'd built rather than permitting its reconstruction, she
would have forfeited a great deal more than the practice itself — namely,
whatever capacity remained in her to become something else worth being.

A former patient recognized her at a conference eighteen months later,
thanking her, somewhat to Camille's surprise, not for the operation itself
but for the unhurried way she had once explained its risks. Camille found
she remembered the conversation clearly, remembered caring, at the time,
about getting the explanation exactly right. It occurred to her only
afterward that this particular skill — patient, careful explanation — had
never actually required a steady hand at all, and had simply been waiting,
unnoticed, for a context in which it might matter on its own.

## New Words

- **cultivate** — to develop or improve something carefully over time
- **disorienting** — causing confusion about one's situation or surroundings
- **unsparing** — not holding back, especially in judgment or criticism
- **hazard** — a source of danger or risk
- **scaffolding** (figurative) — a temporary support structure for something
  being built
- **dexterity** — skill in performing tasks, especially with the hands
- **seamless** — smooth and without noticeable interruption
- **forfeit** — to lose or give up something as a consequence
