# The Apprentice

Eleanor had trained apprentices before, in the twenty-two years since she'd
taken over her father's furniture workshop, but none, she would later
concede, had unsettled her quite so thoroughly as Adrian did during his
first year under her instruction. Not because he lacked aptitude — if
anything, his natural feel for the material outstripped what she recalled of
her own early efforts — but because that very talent seemed, perversely, to
have bred in him a carelessness with the fundamentals she considered
non-negotiable.

He would arrive at elegant solutions to joinery problems that had taken her
years to master, solutions that were, by any technical measure, sound, yet
arrived at via a process so intuitive that he could rarely articulate
afterward how he had reached them. This troubled her more than incompetence
would have. Incompetence, at least, she knew how to correct. Untutored
brilliance, by contrast, tended to plateau precisely at the point where its
possessor first encountered a problem instinct alone could not solve.

"You're relying on a gift that will eventually run out," she told him, after
watching him improvise his way, successfully but recklessly, through a
commission that ought to have demanded considerably more deliberation than
he'd afforded it. "When it does, and it will, you'll have nothing beneath it
to fall back on."

He resisted this assessment for longer than she'd anticipated, convinced, as
talented young people often are, that rules existed primarily for those less
naturally gifted than himself. It was not until he attempted, against her
explicit advice, a complex commission entirely on his own initiative — and
watched it fail in a manner his instincts had never previously permitted —
that something in his resistance finally gave way.

Had she simply forbidden the attempt outright, she suspects the lesson would
never have taken hold; there is a kind of learning, she has come to believe,
that survives no substitute for the failure itself. What she offered
instead, once the disappointment had settled, was not reproach but a
considerably more patient explanation than she might once have thought he
deserved: why the fundamentals existed, what problems they had been
developed, often through generations of accumulated failure, to prevent.

The apprentice who emerged from that period bore little resemblance to the
one who'd entered it, chastened in a manner that, far from diminishing his
evident gift, seemed instead to have given it somewhere solid to stand.
Rarely has she felt prouder of a student's progress than she did watching
him, some eighteen months later, correct a junior apprentice's error with
precisely the patient rigor she had once struggled to instill in him.

When she finally offered him his own workshop, three years into his
training, he asked her, half joking, whether she thought he was actually
ready. She considered the question with more seriousness than his tone had
invited. "No one is entirely ready," she told him. "You are, however, ready
enough to fail usefully, which is rather more than I could say for you a
year ago, and rather more than most people manage before they're forty."
He took this, correctly, as the highest compliment she was likely to offer.

## New Words

- **aptitude** — a natural ability or talent for something
- **perversely** — in a way contrary to what is expected or reasonable
- **joinery** — the craft of constructing wooden joints and fittings
- **plateau** (verb) — to stop improving after a period of progress
- **initiative** — the ability to act independently, without being told
- **chastened** — made humbler or more careful after a failure
- **instill** — to gradually establish a quality or attitude in someone
- **rigor** — strict care, thoroughness, and precision
