# The Weight of Small Things

Beatrice had anticipated, in the abstract way one anticipates any
significant grief before actually encountering its particulars, that
clearing her mother's house would be difficult. What she had not
anticipated, standing in the kitchen three days after the funeral
confronted by a drawer of mismatched cutlery she could not bring herself to
simply donate, was the specific, almost absurd character that difficulty
would assume: that the largest emotional obstacles would prove to be not
the furniture or the photographs, whose significance she had braced for in
advance, but a chaotic accumulation of objects so mundane that their
capacity to devastate her felt, in the moment, faintly ridiculous.

A single wooden spoon, its handle worn smooth by four decades of ordinary
use, produced in her a wave of grief so disproportionate to the object's
evident value that she found herself sitting on the kitchen floor,
spoon in hand, unable for several minutes to proceed with anything
resembling the efficient, systematic sorting she had promised herself she
would maintain throughout the process.

Her brother Desmond, arriving later that afternoon to help with what he had
optimistically imagined would be a straightforward logistical task,
discovered her still there among boxes barely a quarter filled, and did not,
to his credit, attempt the easy reassurance that might have felt, in that
moment, like a dismissal of something she was only beginning to
understand herself.

"I don't know why it's the spoon," she admitted, somewhat sheepishly, once
she had recovered sufficient composure to attempt an explanation. "Not the
wedding photographs. Not even her reading glasses, which I thought would
undo me completely. Just this stupid spoon she used every single day of my
childhood without either of us ever once remarking on it."

"Maybe that's exactly why," Desmond offered, settling onto the floor beside
her rather than standing over her with the brisk efficiency he had arrived
intending to deploy. "The photographs were always occasions. Things she
consciously preserved because she knew, even then, they'd matter later. The
spoon was never meant to matter at all. It was just there, every day, doing
something completely unremarkable, which means it's the truest evidence we
have of what her actual, ordinary life was like — not the version curated
for photographs, but the thousands of unrecorded, forgettable mornings that
made up the overwhelming majority of it."

She found his observation, offered with none of the polish that might have
made it feel rehearsed, unexpectedly consoling — not because it dissolved
the grief the spoon continued, absurdly, to provoke, but because it
reframed that grief as evidence of something worth honoring rather than a
disproportionate response she ought to feel embarrassed about. They spent
the remainder of the afternoon sorting more slowly than either had
intended, pausing periodically over objects neither could have predicted
in advance would matter, gradually assembling not merely a house cleared for
sale but something closer to an accidental, unglamorous inventory of an
entire unremarkable, sufficient life.

She kept the spoon, in the end, along with a handful of other objects
neither of them could adequately justify keeping on any grounds a stranger
would recognize as rational. It sits now in her own kitchen drawer, among
utensils considerably newer and, by any objective measure, more useful,
doing precisely nothing except existing, occasionally, as a small and
entirely sufficient reminder of an ordinary life she is glad, on balance,
to have witnessed as closely as she did.
