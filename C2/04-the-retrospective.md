# The Retrospective

Desmond had spent four decades producing sculpture that, by his own
private estimation, had only occasionally approached the standard he set
for himself, and so it was with a peculiar, almost vertiginous
disorientation that he found himself, at seventy-three, wandering the
opening night of a museum retrospective devoted entirely to a body of work
he had never regarded, in aggregate, as anything more than a long,
imperfect apprenticeship to a mastery he suspected he would die still
approaching.

The curators had arranged the pieces chronologically, a decision he had
initially resisted, fearing it would expose too starkly the false starts and
abandoned directions that populated any honest accounting of a working
life. What he had not anticipated was how differently the work read when
encountered as a continuous arc rather than as the disconnected,
individually adjudicated failures and modest successes he had experienced
in real time, each piece emerging from its own private crucible of doubt
before being released, with considerable relief, into the indifference of
the world.

Walking the galleries, he found himself confronting a version of his own
career that felt simultaneously familiar and entirely foreign — familiar in
its particulars, each piece summoning with total clarity the specific
anxieties and technical obsessions that had consumed him while making it;
foreign in the coherence the retrospective's sequencing imposed upon
choices that had, at the time, felt almost entirely improvised, contingent
on circumstance, financial necessity, and whatever material happened to be
available rather than any grand unifying vision the wall text now
attributed to him with such confident authority.

His daughter, Miriam, accompanying him through the final galleries, noticed
his uncharacteristic silence and asked, gently, what precisely he was
thinking. He considered the question longer than its ordinary weight
warranted.

"I keep waiting to feel the satisfaction everyone assumes this evening
should provide," he admitted eventually. "Instead I mostly feel as though
I'm attending the funeral of a man I worked beside for forty years without
ever quite getting to know him properly. The retrospective is more coherent
than I ever was. I'm not sure that's entirely a compliment to either of us."

Miriam, who had grown up watching her father's private uncertainty
coexist, often uneasily, with his considerable public reputation, offered
neither easy reassurance nor false modesty on his behalf. "Maybe coherence
isn't something you're supposed to feel while you're still inside the
work," she said. "Maybe it's only visible afterward, to people standing far
enough away to see the whole shape rather than just the piece in front of
them." He found he had no immediate rebuttal to this, and permitted
himself, for perhaps the first time that evening, something approaching
genuine pride — not in the work displayed around him, exactly, but in
having produced, alongside it and largely without noticing, a daughter
capable of that particular clarity.

He thought about her observation for weeks afterward, returning to it
whenever he found himself, in the studio, second-guessing a new piece for
lacking the coherence the retrospective had so convincingly imposed on
everything preceding it. Eventually he stopped trying to resolve the
tension and simply let it sit alongside the work itself, unclaimed and
unresolved — proof, he decided, that he had not yet finished being
surprised by what he made, which struck him, at seventy-three, as
considerably better news than the alternative.

## New Words

- **vertiginous** — causing a sensation of dizziness, often figuratively
  overwhelming
- **apprenticeship** (figurative) — a period of learning and developing
  skill through practice
- **crucible** (figurative) — a difficult situation that produces something
  new through pressure
- **coherence** — the quality of being logically connected and consistent
- **uncharacteristic** — not typical of a person's usual behavior
- **modesty** — a moderate view of one's own achievements or importance
