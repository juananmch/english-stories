# Between Languages

Nathaniel had lived in three countries and spoken two languages fluently
enough, for most of his adult life, to be mistaken for a native speaker in
either — an accomplishment he had once regarded with some pride and had
come, in recent years, to experience as something closer to a quiet,
persistent form of dislocation.

The difficulty resisted easy articulation, which was, he sometimes thought
with a certain rueful irony, itself symptomatic of the problem. In English,
the language of his childhood, he possessed an instinctive command of
register and nuance that allowed him to modulate a sentence's tone with an
ease he never had to consciously engineer. In Portuguese, acquired in
adulthood and now the language of his marriage, his household, and the
better part of his waking life, he had achieved a fluency that native
speakers rarely questioned — and yet he remained aware, in ways he could not
entirely suppress, of a faint scaffolding beneath even his most spontaneous
utterances, a residual awareness of construction that never fully
disappeared into unselfconscious speech.

What troubled him was not the linguistic gap itself, which he had long since
made peace with as an unavoidable feature of adult language acquisition, but
its unexpected bleeding into something considerably more fundamental: a
sense that he inhabited, depending on which language he happened to be
occupying at a given moment, subtly different versions of himself, neither
of which felt entirely complete without access to the other.

His wife, Beatrice, a linguist by training and considerably more sanguine
about the whole phenomenon, found his distress faintly amusing, though not
unsympathetically so. "You're describing bilingualism," she told him one
evening, "as though you've diagnosed yourself with a rare condition. Every
person who has ever genuinely inhabited two languages recognizes exactly
what you're describing. The self you access in each language is shaped by
when you learned it, whom you learned it from, what you were doing with your
life at the time. That isn't fragmentation. It's simply what it means to
contain multitudes fluently rather than just poetically."

He found the reframing genuinely useful, if not entirely dissolving of the
underlying disquiet — the sense, which he suspected might never fully
resolve, that some essential continuity of self, taken for granted by
monolingual people without their ever having cause to examine it, had been
irrevocably complicated by the very achievement he had spent decades
cultivating. There were, he had come to accept, no words in either language
that captured this particular in-between state with total precision, which
struck him, on reflection, as perhaps the most honest evidence yet that the
condition was real: a feeling exact enough to be lived and imprecise enough
to resist translation in either direction.

He mentioned this to his daughter, who was growing up trilingual almost
without effort, absorbing Portuguese, English, and her schoolyard's French
with none of the deliberate labor he remembered from his own adult
acquisition. She looked at him, faintly puzzled by the question, when he
asked which language felt most like her real self. "That's like asking
which hand is my real hand," she said, already losing interest and
returning to her homework. He found, turning her answer over on the walk
home, that he envied her more than he was entirely comfortable admitting.

## New Words

- **dislocation** (figurative) — a sense of being displaced or disconnected
  from where one belongs
- **rueful** — expressing regret in a wry, self-aware way
- **modulate** — to adjust the tone or level of something, especially speech
- **residual** — remaining after most of something has gone or been used
- **utterance** — something spoken; an act of speaking
- **bleed** (figurative) — to spread gradually and unintentionally into
  another area
- **fragmentation** — the process of breaking into separate, disconnected
  parts
- **multitude** — a large number of things, or (figuratively) many
  identities within one person
- **disquiet** — a feeling of unease or anxiety
- **irrevocably** — in a way that cannot be reversed or undone
