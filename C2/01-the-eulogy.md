# The Eulogy

Miriam had drafted seven versions of the eulogy before she finally accepted
that no configuration of sentences would resolve the fundamental
difficulty: that the man she was expected to eulogize, and the man she had
actually known, were not quite the same person, and that most of the
mourners gathering tomorrow had met only the former.

Her father had cultivated, across five decades in public life, a persona of
almost frictionless amiability — the sort of man eulogies are written for
effortlessly, full of anecdotes about his generosity, his wit, the ease with
which he charmed strangers into lifelong allegiance. All of this was, in its
way, perfectly true. It was also, Miriam knew with the particular clarity
grief sometimes affords, a curated exhibit rather than a complete account,
one that had required, behind closed doors, a rather more exacting and
occasionally merciless private self to maintain.

She did not intend to eviscerate his memory at his own funeral; the thought
had never seriously tempted her. What troubled her was subtler: the
prospect of standing before several hundred people and delivering a portrait
so thoroughly sanded of its actual contours that her own children, watching,
would learn nothing true about the grandfather whose absence they were, in
their limited way, already beginning to register.

Her brother Desmond, considerably more sanguine about such distinctions,
counseled the path of least resistance. "Nobody wants nuance at a funeral,"
he told her, not unkindly, over the phone the night before. "They want to
grieve the man they thought they knew. Save the complicated version for
somewhere it can actually be heard."

She understood the wisdom in this, and suspected, moreover, that he was
substantially correct about what the occasion could bear. And yet something
in her resisted the idea that honesty and occasion were so neatly
severable — that grief, of all things, should be the context in which a
person's most carefully maintained fiction went unexamined one final time.

What she settled upon, in the small hours before the service, was not
confession but suggestion: an anecdote, ostensibly charming, about her
father's insistence on perfection in the smallest domestic matters, told
with just enough specificity that anyone who had actually lived alongside
that insistence would recognize its double edge, while those who had only
encountered its public, more forgivable expression would hear nothing but
an endearing idiosyncrasy.

It was, she reflected afterward, watching mourners nod approvingly at a
story that had, for her, carried an entirely different weight, perhaps the
truest thing she could offer without demolishing something several hundred
people had gathered, quite reasonably, to preserve. Whether her father, whose
own instinct for calibrated disclosure she had evidently inherited more
thoroughly than she cared to admit, would have approved of the approach, she
found she could not honestly say — though she suspected, with something
between amusement and unease, that he probably would have.

Desmond found her afterward, near the door, accepting condolences with the
practiced composure she had spent the entire service maintaining. "You
found a way to say it," he said quietly, not quite a question. She did not
ask how he had recognized what the anecdote actually contained; she
suspected, watching him, that he had inherited a version of the same
instinct their father had passed down to them both, however differently
each of them had learned to wield it.

## New Words

- **eulogize** — to praise someone highly, especially the deceased at a
  funeral
- **persona** — a public image or character a person presents to others
- **amiability** — the quality of being pleasant and easy to like
- **allegiance** — loyal support for a person, cause, or group
- **eviscerate** (figurative) — to deprive something of its essential
  substance, often through harsh criticism
- **severable** — able to be separated or divided cleanly
- **idiosyncrasy** — a peculiar or distinctive habit unique to a person
- **calibrated** — carefully adjusted to produce a precise, intended effect
- **disclosure** — the act of revealing information, especially something
  private
