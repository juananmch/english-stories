# The Last Correction

Theo had proofread manuscripts for thirty-one years with a fastidiousness
that had, over that span, acquired something approaching legend within the
small, insular world of literary publishing — an eye reputed to catch
errors that had survived three prior rounds of professional scrutiny, an
instinct for the subtle infelicities of rhythm and syntax that separated
competent prose from genuinely well-made sentences. It was this reputation,
built with such deliberate patience across three decades, that made the
email from his publisher that morning so quietly devastating, notwithstanding
its scrupulously diplomatic phrasing.

The firm was transitioning, the letter explained, to an automated editorial
system capable of flagging errors with a consistency and speed no human
proofreader could hope to match, at a fraction of the associated cost. His
services, while acknowledged with evident sincerity as exemplary, would no
longer be required for the majority of the firm's output, though he would,
the letter assured him, remain available for the small handful of prestige
literary projects where a human sensibility was still deemed, for now,
commercially defensible.

He read the email four times, less out of disbelief than out of a
professional habit he found himself unable to suspend even in his own
moment of obsolescence, searching, almost involuntarily, for the sentence's
weaker constructions. There were several. He noted them with the same
detached precision he might have applied to any other manuscript, a
reflex that struck him, once he recognized what he was doing, as either
profoundly absurd or the truest possible testament to what three decades of
disciplined attention does to a mind — it does not simply switch off when
the professional context that justified it disappears.

His colleague Rosalind, hearing the news over lunch, offered the consolation
that his skill, unlike certain more mechanical competencies, could not be
straightforwardly replicated, only imitated at a coarser resolution. "What
you do isn't just error detection," she told him. "It's judgment. A
machine can flag an inconsistency. It cannot tell you whether breaking a
rule, in this particular sentence, serves the writer's actual intention
better than obeying it would."

He appreciated the sentiment, and suspected it was substantially true, and
found himself, nonetheless, unpersuaded that truth alone would prove
sufficient armor against an economic calculation that had already been
made and would, in all likelihood, be made again by other firms watching
this one's costs decline. What remained unresolved, turning the question
over during the walk home, was not whether his judgment retained genuine
value — he believed, without false modesty, that it did — but whether a
world increasingly optimized for measurable efficiency would continue
to consider that value worth paying for, once a merely adequate
alternative existed at a fraction of the price so small as to render
argument almost beside the point. It was, he reflected, a question
considerably larger than his own career, though he found it arrived, as
such questions typically do, disguised as something entirely personal.

He accepted the reduced arrangement, in the end, telling himself it was
merely practical rather than any form of concession, though he suspected
Rosalind saw through the distinction easily enough. The handful of literary
manuscripts he continued to receive felt, in the months that followed,
different from the routine work he had lost — not busier, exactly, but
weightier, as though each one now carried some small portion of an argument
he was quietly still making, sentence by sentence, for why the work had
mattered in the first place.

## New Words

- **fastidiousness** — extreme attention to detail and accuracy
- **insular** — relating to a narrow, closed community, isolated from
  outsiders
- **infelicity** — an unsuitable or awkward expression in speech or writing
- **exemplary** — serving as an excellent example; outstanding
- **obsolescence** — the state of becoming outdated or no longer needed
- **testament** (figurative) — something that serves as clear evidence of a
  quality or fact
- **armor** (figurative) — protection against criticism, harm, or difficulty
