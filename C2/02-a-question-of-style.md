# A Question of Style

Rosalind had edited Theo's novels for over two decades, a professional
relationship that had, somewhere along the way and without either of them
quite marking the transition, ripened into something closer to friendship
than commerce typically allows. It was precisely this intimacy that made
the manuscript now sitting on her desk so agonizing to confront honestly.

The prose was, by any dispassionate measure, diminished — not incompetent,
exactly, since Theo remained incapable of writing an outright bad sentence,
but conspicuously less alive than the work that had established his
reputation. The sentences that had once seemed to arrive at their own
inevitable shape now bore the faint, unmistakable fingerprints of
effort: careful, competent, and curiously inert, like a portrait painted
from photographs rather than a sitting.

She had encountered this phenomenon before, in other writers whose gifts had
quietly begun to recede while their technical facility, paradoxically,
sharpened — as though craft accumulated precisely in inverse proportion to
whatever ineffable quality had made the earlier work matter in the first
place. She did not know, with any confidence, whether this constituted
simple fatigue, the accumulated weight of two decades' scrutiny finally
exacting its toll, or something considerably more difficult to name and
harder still to reverse.

What complicated matters further was the near-certainty that Theo already
suspected as much himself, and was submitting the manuscript, at least in
part, in the hope that she would either confirm or dispel a fear he lacked
the detachment to adjudicate alone. An editor less invested in the
friendship might have hedged, offering the customary diplomatic
notes — pacing here, a subplot there — while privately resolving to let the
book find its own middling reception without further comment. Rosalind
found she could not quite bring herself to extend him that particular
courtesy, suspecting, not unreasonably, that it would constitute its own
species of abandonment.

She wrote to him plainly, in the end, choosing candor over the various more
comfortable evasions available to her. The letter did not itemize failures
so much as name, as precisely as she was able, the quality she found
missing — a certain willingness to risk incoherence that the earlier books
had possessed in abundance and this one, for whatever reason, did not.

His reply, when it arrived some days later, was shorter than she expected
and considerably less wounded. "I have been waiting eighteen months for
someone to say exactly that," he wrote. "Everyone else has been extraordinarily
kind, which turns out to have been the problem." Whether this admission
would translate into a genuinely different book, or merely into a period of
uncomfortable soul-searching without resolution, Rosalind could not say with
certainty. She knew only that the alternative — continued, comfortable
dishonesty, dressed up as professional tact — had never, in her considerable
experience, produced anything worth the paper it occupied.

He sent her the opening chapters of a revision three months later, with a
note that read only: "Tell me honestly. I would rather hear it now than
after another eighteen months." She read them twice before replying, taking
the second reading more slowly than the first, less to confirm her
impression than to be certain she was not simply relieved to find what she
was hoping for. What she found, to her considerable satisfaction, was
something closer to the earlier books than anything he had shown her in
years — not perfect, not yet fully realized, but unmistakably, gratifyingly
alive again in a way careful competence alone had never quite managed to
fake.

## New Words

- **ripen** (figurative) — to develop gradually into a fuller, more mature
  state
- **adjudicate** — to make a formal judgment about a disputed matter
- **evasion** — an act of avoiding something directly, especially a question
  or duty
- **incoherence** — a lack of logical connection or clarity
- **wounded** (figurative) — emotionally hurt, especially by criticism
- **tact** — sensitivity in dealing with others, especially in difficult
  matters
- **gratifyingly** — in a way that produces satisfaction or pleasure
