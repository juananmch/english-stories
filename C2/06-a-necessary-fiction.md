# A Necessary Fiction

Beatrice had practiced palliative medicine for nineteen years, long enough
to have developed a fluency in a particular register of speech that her
younger colleagues, still adjusting to the discipline's peculiar demands,
frequently mistook for evasiveness rather than what it actually was: a
carefully calibrated instrument, refined through long experience, for
conveying difficult truths at precisely the pace and specificity a given
patient could productively absorb.

The distinction mattered enormously to her, though she recognized it was
not always legible from the outside. There was a considerable difference,
she had come to believe, between lying to a dying patient and declining to
volunteer information whose only likely effect would be to compound
suffering without altering outcome — between false reassurance, which she
regarded as a genuine ethical failure, and a deliberate, judicious
withholding of certainty in circumstances where certainty itself remained
genuinely unavailable, medicine being, whatever its pretensions to
precision, considerably less exact a discipline than most patients
assumed.

Her newest resident, Nathaniel, brought to this work an admirable but as
yet unrefined commitment to what he termed radical honesty, a principle he
applied with a rigor Beatrice recognized, not unsympathetically, as the
particular zealotry of the recently converted. He had, that week, informed
a frightened patient of a statistical prognosis with a bluntness that,
while not technically inaccurate, had stripped away every contextual
nuance that might have rendered the information bearable rather than
simply devastating.

"You told her the truth," Beatrice acknowledged, reviewing the encounter
with him afterward, "and I don't doubt your intentions were admirable. But
truth delivered without regard for what a person can actually metabolize in
a given moment isn't honesty. It's just unprocessed data, and you've
essentially asked a frightened, exhausted woman to do the interpretive work
that was, in fact, your job to do for her."

Nathaniel, to his credit, did not retreat into easy defensiveness, though he
pushed back with a fair question: at what point did such calibration risk
sliding into the paternalism the profession had, over previous generations,
worked hard to move beyond? It was not, Beatrice conceded, an unreasonable
concern, nor one she considered fully resolved even after two decades of
practice. The line between compassionate pacing and outright
condescension was neither fixed nor, in her experience, reliably
discoverable in advance; it had to be renegotiated, patient by patient,
often moment by moment, guided by attention rather than by any general
rule sturdy enough to survive every circumstance it might encounter.

"There is no formula," she told him finally, "that spares you from having to
actually look at the person in front of you and decide, imperfectly, what
they need to hear today rather than everything that happens to be true. That
uncertainty isn't a flaw in the profession. It is, more or less, the entire
profession."

Nathaniel returned to the patient's room the following morning, uncertain
what, precisely, he intended to say, only that leaving the previous day's
bluntness unrevisited felt like its own quiet failure. He did not apologize,
exactly, sensing correctly that an apology would center his own discomfort
rather than her needs. He simply sat, asked what questions had stayed with
her overnight, and let the conversation proceed at whatever pace she, rather
than his own anxious thoroughness, required. It was, he recognized only
afterward, the first time all week he had practiced anything resembling
what Beatrice had actually been trying to teach him.

## New Words

- **palliative** — relating to care that relieves suffering rather than
  curing illness
- **evasiveness** — a tendency to avoid giving direct or complete answers
- **legible** (figurative) — clearly understandable or interpretable
- **compound** (verb) — to make a problem or difficulty worse
- **zealotry** — excessive or fanatical enthusiasm for a cause
- **prognosis** — a medical forecast of how a disease will develop
- **metabolize** (figurative) — to process and absorb information or
  experience emotionally
- **paternalism** — the practice of limiting someone's freedom for their
  perceived own good
- **condescension** — an attitude of patronizing superiority toward others

## Questions

   certainty?

4. What fair question did Nathaniel push back with?
5. What did Beatrice conclude there was no formula for?
