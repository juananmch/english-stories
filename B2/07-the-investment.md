# The Investment

Had Felix known how the following year would unfold, he almost certainly
would have said no the moment his old university friend first mentioned the
opportunity. Instead, flattered to have been asked and reassured by
confident-sounding projections, he invested a significant portion of his
savings into a startup he barely understood.

The friend, Thomas, was persuasive in the way that people who genuinely
believe their own optimism often are. The company, still in its early stages,
was developing software that, according to Thomas, would inevitably disrupt
an entire industry within two years. Felix, having watched several colleagues
profit substantially from similarly early investments, found the argument
more convincing than perhaps it deserved to be.

For the first six months, things appeared to be proceeding reasonably well.
Updates from Thomas were consistently optimistic, peppered with phrases like
"strong momentum" and "imminent breakthrough." Felix, choosing not to examine
the figures too closely, allowed himself to be reassured.

The first warning sign, when it came, was subtle: a delayed update, followed
by a vaguer explanation than usual regarding a "minor restructuring." Felix,
uneasy but not yet alarmed, asked for more detail. The response was slower and
less specific than he would have liked.

By the time the full picture emerged — a key contract having fallen through,
funding having dried up faster than anticipated — it was considerably worse
than Felix had feared. The company folded within weeks. His investment,
substantial though not catastrophic, was almost entirely lost.

What troubled him most, afterward, wasn't strictly the money, though that
stung considerably. It was the recognition that he had ignored several
instincts along the way, choosing reassurance over scrutiny because
scrutiny would have been uncomfortable, particularly given his friendship
with Thomas.

"I should have asked harder questions," he admitted to his wife. "I think
part of me didn't want to know the answers."

He didn't blame Thomas entirely; he suspected, in fact, that Thomas had
genuinely believed everything he'd said, right up until it collapsed. But the
experience left him considerably more cautious, not merely about
investments, but about how readily he allowed enthusiasm — his own included —
to substitute for actual diligence.

Months later, Thomas reached out, apologetic and visibly embarrassed, offering
to repay what he could over time even though he had no legal obligation to do
so. Felix declined, somewhat to his own surprise. "You didn't take anything
from me that I didn't hand over myself," he told him. "I'd rather we stayed
friends than spend the next few years arguing about money neither of us can
really get back." It wasn't entirely generosity; it was also, he suspected, a
way of finally letting the whole episode go.

Thomas thanked him, clearly relieved, though also a little uncertain what to
do with a debt that had just been forgiven rather than repaid. They didn't
talk about the money again after that. When Thomas eventually started another
company, years later, he didn't ask Felix to invest, and Felix didn't offer.
Neither of them ever said so directly, but both understood it was simply a
line they had already learned not to cross a second time.

## New Words

- **flatter** — to make someone feel good by praising them, sometimes
  insincerely
- **projection** — a prediction based on current information or trends
- **restructuring** — a significant reorganization of a company or system
- **catastrophic** — extremely harmful or damaging
- **diligence** — careful and thorough effort or investigation
