# The Mentor

For most of her career, Isabel had assumed that being a good mentor meant
having good answers. It was only after several years of mentoring junior
staff, Henry among them, that she began to suspect she had been getting
something fundamental wrong.

Henry had joined her team two years earlier, ambitious but visibly anxious,
the kind of employee who apologized before asking questions and second-guessed
almost every decision he made. Isabel, recognizing something of her younger
self in him, had taken a particular interest in his development, offering
advice generously whenever he seemed uncertain.

The trouble, she eventually realized, was that her advice, however
well-intentioned, had subtly encouraged a dependency neither of them
consciously noticed. Whenever a problem arose, Henry brought it to her before
attempting to solve it himself, and she, flattered to be needed and genuinely
enjoying the role, rarely redirected him elsewhere.

The moment that forced a reconsideration was small: Henry, faced with a
straightforward decision she would ordinarily have made for him, hesitated
for so long waiting for her input that a minor opportunity was missed
entirely. Watching this unfold, Isabel felt something closer to alarm than
disappointment.

"If I hadn't made myself so available," she admitted to a colleague
afterward, "I don't think this would have happened. I think I taught him to
wait for me rather than trust himself."

She approached the next conversation with Henry differently. Rather than
offering a solution when he arrived with a problem, she asked what he thought
the options were, and, more deliberately, resisted the urge to fill the
silence that followed.

It was uncomfortable, initially, for both of them. Henry, unused to being
asked rather than told, visibly struggled with the shift, and Isabel, watching
him hesitate, had to consciously stop herself from rescuing the conversation
the way she instinctively wanted to.

Gradually, though, something changed. Henry began arriving with tentative
proposals rather than open questions, and while his early suggestions weren't
always right, the act of proposing them seemed to matter more than whether
they succeeded.

Months later, he made a decision entirely on his own that turned out well,
and mentioned it to Isabel almost as an afterthought.

"I didn't think you'd need to hear about that one," he said.

"That," Isabel told him, "is exactly the point."

That evening, writing notes for her own performance review, Isabel found
herself reconsidering how she described her management style. She had always
listed "hands-on support" as one of her strengths, and for the first time she
wondered whether that phrase, however well-intentioned, had actually been
describing a habit worth breaking rather than one worth celebrating. She
rewrote the line twice before settling on something closer to what she
actually meant: that her real contribution, increasingly, was learning when
not to help.

## New Words

- **mentor** — someone who advises and guides a less experienced person
- **dependency** — a state of relying heavily on someone or something
- **redirect** — to send or guide toward a different direction or source
- **reconsideration** — the act of thinking about something again, often
  leading to change
- **input** — advice, opinions, or information contributed to a decision
- **deliberately** — done on purpose, intentionally
- **instinctively** — done automatically, without conscious thought
- **proposal** — a suggestion or plan put forward for consideration
- **afterthought** — something added or thought of later, almost as a minor
  detail
