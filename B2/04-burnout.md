# Burnout

Looking back, Henry could identify the exact week things had started to
unravel, though at the time it hadn't felt like a single moment so much as a
slow accumulation of small failures to say no.

He had taken on the new project in March, telling himself it was temporary,
that he would simply push through until the deadline and then things would
return to normal. Having already been working late most evenings, he added
weekend hours without really deciding to; it simply became what he did.

By June, he was sleeping badly, forgetting things he wouldn't normally
forget. It wasn't like him to snap at his partner, Isabel, but by then he was
doing it often, usually over matters that didn't deserve it. She had
mentioned her concern more than once, gently at first, then with increasing
urgency.

"You're not actually present anymore," she told him one evening. "Even when
you're here, you're somewhere else."

He wanted to disagree, but found he couldn't, not honestly. Having spent the
entire dinner distracted by an email he hadn't yet answered, he recognized,
uncomfortably, that she was right.

The moment that finally forced a reckoning was smaller than he expected: he
forgot his daughter's school event entirely, something he would never
ordinarily have done, and the guilt that followed was disproportionate to the
mistake itself, precisely because it revealed how far things had drifted.

He booked time with a doctor, expecting to be told he simply needed more
sleep. Instead, the conversation turned to burnout — not as a vague concept,
but as something with recognizable symptoms he had been dismissing for
months: the exhaustion that didn't improve with rest, the irritability, the
sense of detachment from work he had once found meaningful.

"This isn't a productivity problem," the doctor told him. "It's a warning
sign. If you keep pushing at this pace, something is going to give, and it
might not be something you can easily fix."

Reluctantly, Henry spoke to his manager, expecting resistance. Instead, he was
offered a reduced workload and, somewhat to his surprise, genuine
understanding. It turned out he wasn't the first person on the team to
struggle in silence, assuming that admitting difficulty would be read as
weakness.

Recovery wasn't immediate. It took months to rebuild the habits he had
abandoned — proper breaks, actual weekends, conversations with Isabel that
weren't interrupted by his phone. But slowly, something that had felt entirely
lost began, gradually, to return.

One evening, several months later, Isabel noticed him closing his laptop at
six without being asked. "Who are you," she said, only half joking, "and what
have you done with my husband?" He laughed, properly, in a way that felt
unfamiliar even to him. It occurred to him afterward that he couldn't
remember the last time laughing hadn't felt like an interruption to something
more urgent, and that realization, small as it was, told him more about how
far he had drifted than any conversation with a doctor ever could.

## New Words

- **accumulation** — a gradual increase through repeated addition
- **snap at** — to speak suddenly and angrily to someone
- **urgency** — the quality of needing immediate attention
- **reckoning** — a moment when a problem must finally be faced
- **detachment** — a feeling of being distant or disconnected from something
- **irritability** — the state of being easily annoyed
- **workload** — the amount of work a person is expected to do
