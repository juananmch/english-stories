# The Job Offer

By the time the email arrived, Marcus had almost forgotten he had applied for
the position. It had been nearly two months since the interview, and he had
assumed the company had chosen someone else.

The subject line said simply: "Job Offer — Regional Manager." He read it
twice before he let himself believe it. The salary was significantly higher
than what he earned now, and the role would be based in another city, three
hours from where he currently lived.

That evening, he told his wife, Grace, over dinner.

"That's incredible," she said. "But it would mean moving. Have you thought
about that part?"

"I have," Marcus admitted. "I don't know what to think yet."

If they moved, Grace would have to leave her job at the local school, where
she had taught for six years and was well respected by both students and
colleagues. Their daughter, who was in her final year of primary school, would
have to change schools too, right before starting secondary school.

"If we didn't have kids," Grace said, "I think this would be an easy decision.
It's a great opportunity for you."

They spent the weekend making lists: the higher salary against the cost of
moving, the new opportunities against everything familiar they would leave
behind. Marcus called his sister, who had relocated for work twice in her
career.

"Both times were hard," she told him honestly. "But I don't regret either
one. You adjust faster than you think you will."

By Sunday evening, they still hadn't reached a decision. Grace suggested
something neither of them had considered: visiting the new city together
before deciding anything.

The following weekend, they drove down. They walked around the neighborhoods,
found a school that seemed promising, and had lunch in a small café where the
owner, overhearing their conversation, told them the area was a wonderful
place to raise a family.

Driving home, Grace reached over and took Marcus's hand.

"I think we should do it," she said.

He smiled, relieved that they had made the decision together rather than
letting fear make it for them. It would still be difficult, he knew, but it no
longer felt like a risk they were taking blindly. It felt like a choice they
had actually made, with their eyes open, and that seemed to matter more than
he had expected.
